The Strong, Silent Type

Odysseus and Machiavelli’s prince use silence as a lever, a strategy kept for enemies and rivals. The screen’s strong, silent man makes it his default way of feeling instead, a performance that always needs someone to watch it, which is why the camera suits him and why self-help can sell his calm back to men.

Fred Xu · November 18, 2026 · 30 min read

In the first episode of The Sopranos, broadcast in January 1999, a New Jersey mobster who has begun to faint from panic attacks sits in a psychiatrist’s office and, having complained that he “came in at the end” and that “the best is over,” delivers a small elegy for a vanished kind of man: “Whatever happened to Gary Cooper? The strong, silent type. That was an American. He wasn’t in touch with his feelings. He just did what he had to do. See, what they didn’t know is once they got Gary Cooper in touch with his feelings, they wouldn’t be able to shut him up.” The joke is in the speaker, for Tony Soprano mourns the silent man in a therapist’s chair and in a flood of words, and the series, which would later give one of its episodes the title “The Strong, Silent Type,” spends six seasons letting him talk. The figure arrives on television, in other words, already as something lost, remembered by a man who knows he cannot be it.

It was the films of the twentieth century, and above all the Western, that made the strong, silent man a type an audience could recognize in a single shot: a tall man of few words, spoken low and slow, whose competence and whose violence are held in reserve until the last reel, who will not explain himself, and who ends, more often than not, outside the home he has made safe. His solitude is not a misfortune that has befallen him but part of what he is, the mark of a man who needs no one, and when he is asked to explain himself, usually by a woman, he answers that a man does what he has to do, which is almost word for word the phrase Tony would use of Cooper.

The genre is suspicious of language itself, treating talk as the weapon of the weak and a trap for the strong, and yet the hero’s silence is partly an effect of contrast, since the townsfolk, the villains and the women talk around him, so that the strong, silent man is defined less by muteness than by a choice of what to leave unsaid and by a register in which the little he says is said.

A man who refuses to explain himself is a standing invitation to interpretation, which is why this essay, like an earlier one of mine on samurai films, is an exercise in hermeneutics rather than in film history. The silent hero’s silence can be read as depth, the completeness of a man who needs no one, or as emptiness, and the question of which it is cannot be settled by the hero, since he will not say. His admirers and his critics alike reach back to antiquity to answer it, to Homer’s Odysseus, to Spartan brevity and above all to the Stoics, whose books are now marketed to men who want to be calm, and so I will set the modern figure beside the ancient sources it claims. Those sources, I will argue, treat silence as a lever that can be pulled in two directions. Odysseus in disguise among the suitors, the laconic Spartans and Machiavelli’s prince use it strategically, among enemies and rivals, for a time and toward an end, while the modern cult of Stoic calm uses it emotionally, as a standing way of meeting grief and fear that can be bought and worn. The strong, silent man of the screen confuses the two, taking a restraint that antiquity kept for enemy ground and making it his manner everywhere, while dropping what antiquity demanded of a man among his own, which was speech, feeling and fellowship, and what presents itself as self-possession turns out, on the evidence of the films themselves, to be a performance that always needs someone to watch it and someone to tell it.

Endure, My Heart

The Greek hero whom the strong, silent man most resembles is Odysseus, whom Homer calls “much-enduring,” and the resemblance lies in a silence that is a strategy rather than a refusal. When Odysseus returns to Ithaca after twenty years, disguised as a beggar, he must sit in his own hall and watch the suitors who are courting his wife and eating his house bare, and when the maids slip out at night to the suitors’ beds his heart growls within him like a dog standing over her pups, and he strikes his breast and says, “Endure, my heart; a worse thing even than this didst thou once endure.” The endurance is not the end of the story but its precondition, for two books later the beggar strings the great bow and kills every suitor in the hall, and the shape of that story, insult swallowed in silence and then repaid in one long outburst of violence, is the shape of countless revenge films since, down to John Wick. Christopher Nolan’s film of the poem, released this summer with Matt Damon as Odysseus, has just brought the patient hero himself back to the screen.

The poem, however, refuses to make its hero a man without words or tears, for Odysseus is also Homer’s great talker, who tells the story of his own wanderings at a king’s table over four books of the poem. At that same table, before he has said who he is, he draws his purple cloak over his face when the bard sings of Troy, as in the lines at the head of this essay, because he is ashamed to let his hosts see him weep. When the bard sings again, of the fall of the city, Homer likens the tears the hero hides to those of a woman who clings to her husband as he dies in front of their city, while the enemy beats her back and shoulders with spears and leads her away into slavery, so that the sacker of cities is compared to a woman of a sacked one. And when Odysseus at last reveals himself to Telemachus, “he kissed his son, and from his cheeks let fall a tear to earth, but before he ever steadfastly held them back,” and father and son wail together “more vehemently than birds” whose young have been taken from the nest. The silence of Odysseus is a disguise worn on enemy ground, and it comes off when he is among his own.

If any Greeks made a public virtue of saying little, it was the Spartans, who have given English its word for the manner, since “laconic” comes from Laconia, their country. Plutarch says that the lawgiver Lycurgus contrived “that the general habit of silence should make the boys sententious and correct in their answers,” and he records the saying of a Spartan named Archidamidas, “He who knows how, knows also when to speak.” The most famous example is a single word. When Philip of Macedon wrote to the Spartans, “If I invade Laconia, I shall turn you out,” they wrote back, “If.” Spartan speech, Plutarch says, “is the fruit of much silence,” and the laconic reply is a weapon that gains its force from what it leaves out, which is exactly the force of the Virginian’s most famous line, as we will see.

Aristotle’s portrait of the great-souled man, the person who claims much because he deserves much, supplies the bearing of the type with uncanny precision. He is “not a gossip,” he is “least of all men given to lamentation or the asking of favours,” and “a slow step is thought proper to the proud man, a deep voice, and a level utterance,” so that one might take the passage for a description of Gary Cooper. But the same chapter of the Nicomachean Ethics forbids the sealed interior that the films would later prize, for the great-souled man “must also be open in his hate and in his love (for to conceal one’s feelings, i.e. to care less for truth than for what people will think, is a coward’s part).” And Aristotle’s brave man feels fear and pain, since “death and wounds will be painful to the brave man and against his will,” so that a hero who feels nothing in the face of death would, by his account, be not brave but reckless. The Greeks, in short, offered the modern type a patient silence and a laconic wit, a deep voice and a level utterance, but they set all of these inside a culture that expected a man to speak and that let even its most cunning hero weep.

What the Stoics Asked of Speech

Of all the ancient schools it is the Stoics who are most often claimed for the strong, silent man, and with some reason, for the Stoic texts contain the most direct warrant for him anywhere in antiquity. Epictetus, a former slave who taught in Greece in the early second century, advised his students, “let silence be the general rule, or let only what is necessary be said, and in few words,” and named the subjects to avoid, which were “gladiators, … horse-races, … athletes, … eating or drinking … and especially not about men, as blaming them or praising them.” His description of the student who is making progress could serve as the biography of Eastwood’s Man with No Name: such a person “says nothing about himself as if he were somebody or knew something; … if a man censures him, he makes no defence.” And his whole ethic could be compressed, his students remembered, into two words that the Roman writer Aulus Gellius preserved as anechou kai apechou, “bear and forbear.”

Marcus Aurelius, the emperor who wrote down his own Stoic exercises in a notebook, gave the ethic its most quoted motto, “No longer talk at all about the kind of man that a good man ought to be, but be such.” He compared the good man to a vine that bears its grapes and “does not call out for others to come and see, but he goes on to another act,” and to a promontory “against which the waves continually break, but it stands firm and tames the fury of the water around it.” Seneca, the statesman and tutor of Nero, wrote to his friend Lucilius that “philosophy teaches us to act, not to speak,” and the three of them together seem to have written the creed of the screen hero centuries before the screen.

Rome had its own saint of taciturnity in the younger Cato, the Stoic senator who later chose suicide rather than accept Caesar’s mercy. Plutarch tells how, as a boy, Cato was held out of an upper window by a visitor who wanted his support and threatened to drop him, and he “said not a word,” and how, as a young man, he answered those who blamed his silence by saying, “Only let them not blame my life. I will begin to speak when I am not going to say what were better off unsaid.” Yet the same Cato, once he entered public life, “often spoke all day without getting tired,” and his silence was a reserve of force kept for the forum, not a refusal of language. Seneca later made him the Stoic exemplar, and when he describes the spectacle most worthy of the gods’ attention he chooses “a brave man matched with evil fortune,” with Cato as his example, “still standing upright amid the ruins of the commonwealth,” so that Stoic endurance was, already in Seneca, something to be watched.

Read more closely, the Stoic texts ask of speech what Cato’s life shows, which is not silence but agreement between words and life. Seneca’s rule in another letter is “let us say what we feel, and feel what we say; let speech harmonize with life,” and a hero who feels much and says nothing fails the first half of it. Epictetus, consoling a friend, permits exactly what the films forbid: “do not be unwilling to show him sympathy, and even … to lament with him. But take care that you do not lament internally also.” The Stoic may weep on the outside so long as he is calm within, while the movie hero is calm on the outside and grieving within, which is the Stoic formula turned precisely inside out. The deeds that Epictetus wants instead of words are domestic and civic deeds, not feats of arms, and when he imagines the philosopher proving his art the way a carpenter proves his, by showing his work, the work he names is to “marry, beget children, do the office of a citizen, endure abuse, bear with an unreasonable brother.” And Marcus, who had more reason than any man alive to perform his self-command, thanked his teacher for showing him how not to display himself “as a man who practises much discipline,” and warned that a good disposition “is invincible if it be genuine, and not an affected smile and acting a part.”

Seneca is plainest of all on grief, writing that to keep “your countenance unmoved at the very funeral ceremony … would mean lack of feeling rather than virtue,” and advising, on the death of a friend, “Let not the eyes be dry when we have lost a friend, nor let them overflow. We may weep, but we must not wail.” He confesses that he himself wept for a friend “excessively,” and he notes, in a sentence that explains a great deal, that Roman custom laid down periods of mourning for women but that “in the case of men, no rules are laid down, because to mourn at all is not regarded as honourable.” The dry-eyed man was a Roman custom, which Seneca recorded, not a Stoic doctrine, which he questioned. The Stoic sage was meant to be free of passions that rest on false judgements, not of feeling as such, and the Stoics even gave names to the good feelings of the wise, which they called joy, caution and rational wishing, so that their sage was never meant to be an unfeeling machine.

Where, then, does the image of the Stoic as the man who grits his teeth and endures come from? It owes more to the Renaissance than to antiquity, and above all to the Flemish humanist Justus Lipsius, whose book On Constancy of 1584 offered Stoicism as armour against the miseries of a continent at war, while the ancient teaching had aimed less at bearing what one feels than at judging it differently, so that nothing is left to grit one’s teeth against. The screen’s stoic, with a small s, is therefore the heir of Roman custom and of an early modern reading more than of the Stoic texts, and it matters for what follows that Stoicism was from the start a philosophy addressed to elite men, to senators and an emperor who had others to do their bidding.

From Cooper to Eastwood

When the cinema took up this inheritance, its founding scene was a speech act about speech. In Wister’s novel a card player named Trampas insults the Virginian with an obscenity the book does not print, and the Virginian, his pistol out and his hand lying on the table “holding it unaimed,” answers, “When you call me that, SMILE,” drawling “a very little more than usual, so that there was almost a space between each word.” The line is a Spartan reply in cowboy dress, an ultimatum whose force lies in what it does not say. Gary Cooper spoke it in the film of 1929, one of the first sound Westerns, as “If you wanna call me that—smile,” and it made him the face of the type for the next thirty years.

In the 1950s Cooper’s reserve became a duty that cannot be explained, for in High Noon (1952) his marshal Will Kane, abandoned by the town he has protected, is asked by his new wife why he will not simply leave, and can only answer, “I’ve got to. That’s the whole thing,” and when the gunfight is over he throws his marshal’s star in the dirt and leaves town with his wife, letting the gesture speak for him.

In the 1960s Sergio Leone and Clint Eastwood made silence an explicit strategy of mystery, and Eastwood asked for his lines to be cut in the Italian Westerns, on the reasoning that every scene of explanation would dissipate the character’s mystery and, with it, his strength. The silence of the Man with No Name, as Eastwood’s character came to be called, was in other words authored, a choice made by an actor who understood that an audience will fill an empty face with meaning. Eastwood’s own late Western gave that face a past, for in Unforgiven (1992), which he also directed, he played an old killer whose silence covers a conscience, and who tells a young gunman shaken by his first killing, “It’s a hell of a thing, killing a man,” and then, “We all have it coming, Kid.” Across these films the silence moves from reserve to mystery to wound, and what the hero keeps silent about is always one of three things, the violence he is capable of, the past he will not tell, and the tenderness he cannot show.

The Lion and the Fox

The best-known modern defender of manliness, the Harvard political philosopher Harvey Mansfield, makes a surprising claim in his book Manliness (2006), that “Stoicism is the philosophy of manliness,” although the manliness he means is aloof and passive, that of a man whose rule is silence and who never draws attention to himself. His most telling remark goes to the heart of this essay: “Perhaps the silent type needs to have his silence noticed — spoken of — by someone else, someone who gives the interpretation and asserts the significance. The drama of manliness needs to be dramatized by a poet.”

Mansfield is also the leading American translator of Machiavelli, a writer Tony Soprano knows secondhand, for “most of the guys that I know, they read Prince Machiavelli,” he tells his psychiatrist, and “I had Carmela go and get the Cliff Notes once.” Machiavelli supplies the counter-voice that the Stoic ideal needs, an account of silence as political rather than emotional, and in the eighteenth chapter of The Prince he says that a ruler must imitate two beasts, since “one needs to be a fox to recognize snares and a lion to frighten the wolves. Those who stay simply with the lion do not understand this.” Read beside Homer, the fox is Odysseus, the man of many wiles, and the strong, silent hero of the films wants to be the lion while living out the fox’s revenge plot. The same chapter overturns the Stoic ideal of being what one is, for “everyone sees how you appear, few touch what you are,” and a ruler need only appear to have the virtues, which is also all that a camera can record of the screen hero, whose stillness is shown to us and never explained.

It is tempting to make Machiavelli simply the man of action against the Stoic man of endurance, the one bending fortune and the other resigned to it, yet his word for excellence, virtù, keeps room for patience and passivity as well as boldness, and the ancient word behind it began in endurance. Cicero explains that “the name ‘virtue’ comes from vir, a man, and courage is the peculiar distinction of a man: and this virtue has two principal duties, to despise death and pain,” so that the Roman root of manliness is endurance, and the modern word for goodness began as a word for the capacity to bear pain without complaint.

The fox has a modern heir in Harry Da Souza, the fixer whom Tom Hardy plays in MobLand (2025), a London crime series created by Ronan Bennett, whose first two episodes Guy Ritchie directed. Harry has every outward mark of the strong, silent type, the stillness, the courtesy, the low voice and the few words, and it is easy to take him for a stoic who bears the violence of the Harrigan crime family he serves without being moved by it, but his composure is a technique rather than an acceptance. In the first episode he intimidates a club owner into erasing the footage from his security cameras; later he blackmails one of the Stevensons’ own men into smuggling guns into their house, and kills a corrupt detective and stages the death as a suicide; and in the second season he telephones a prisoner to warn him of a coming attack, so that the man will be moved to solitary confinement, and then bribes a prison manager and pays two inmates to kill him there, so that the warning that looks like mercy is the snare itself.

Harry’s silence works the way Machiavelli says a prince’s virtues should work, by being seen. It makes his rare threats carry, as when his sincerity and a single unnerving warning silence his daughter and Eddie Harrigan, who have been taunting him; it makes him the man whom rivals prefer to deal with; and it lets everyone around him believe they know what he is. Hardy described the character in the same terms, “Harry is Harry depending on who he is with,” which is the fox’s principle that everyone sees how you appear and few touch what you are. If the screen hero wants to be the lion while living out the fox’s plot, Harry is the fox who has learned to wear the lion’s face, and the Stoic’s, and the series knows what that face costs at home, where his warning to his wife that she is being manipulated is the one thing he says that she will not believe.

Wound and Sovereignty

After 1990 the silent man splits in two, and in one branch his silence is a wound, the sign of a grief or a guilt too large to speak, while in the other it is pure sovereignty, the calm of a man who needs nothing and no one. Nicolas Winding Refn’s Drive (2011) belongs to the first, and it was cut toward silence by design, for at Ryan Gosling’s suggestion that the Driver should speak only when asked something specific, Refn kept removing dialogue until a script of eighty-one pages had shrunk to about sixty, and he dressed his hero in a satin jacket with a scorpion on the back that serves him as armour. The film can be read either way, as the chivalry of a lonely knight or as moods standing in for emotions, which is the choice between depth and emptiness with which this essay began.

The John Wick films carry the economy further, for by one count Keanu Reeves speaks some 380 words in the whole of the fourth film, and what the silence hides is stated in the first one, where Wick explains that “when Helen died, I lost everything,” and that the puppy his dying wife left him, which a gangster’s son kills, was “an opportunity to grieve unalone,” so that in this branch the Stoic endurance of the old Westerns has been bent toward Odyssean revenge.

The second branch is best represented by Lee Child’s Jack Reacher, the drifter of some thirty novels, two films and a television series, whose catchphrase on the page is “Reacher said nothing.” Child set out to avoid the damaged hero whose wounds explain him, and he has been frank that Reacher exists to answer the reader’s wishes, so that his silence hides nothing and he is the purest modern case of the sovereign man, needing no home and no one, at ease in every fight.

Other films of the period make the silence itself their subject and show who pays for it, as in Brokeback Mountain (2005), where Ennis Del Mar, whose father made him look as a boy at the body of a rancher beaten to death for living with another man, cannot speak his love for Jack, and the lesson that taught him to fear other men taught him to fear his own feelings. In Manchester by the Sea (2016) a man destroyed by the death of his children can tell his nephew only, “I can’t beat it. I can’t beat it. I’m sorry.” Television, with its seasons of screen time, is the medium most able to make the silent man talk, and David Chase has said that therapists noticed a rise in male clients after The Sopranos.

Calm for Sale

Where Machiavelli’s silence is a political lever, the silence sold by the modern Stoic revival is an emotional and cultural one, and the revival has a founder in the marketing writer Ryan Holiday, whose The Obstacle Is the Way (2014) and The Daily Stoic (2016) grew into a newsletter, a podcast and a shop. By 2020 the shop was selling a memento mori medallion for 26andaringfor26 and a ring for 26andaringfor245, and the Daily Stoic’s website now invites its readers to “Join 900,000+ other Stoics.” Holiday’s own definition of his subject, given to Sports Illustrated in 2015, is unusually frank: “It’s not a set of ethics or principles. It’s a collection of spiritual exercises designed to help people through the difficulty of life. To focus on managing emotion; specifically, non-helpful emotion.” The reduction had begun earlier, in psychotherapy, when the founders of cognitive therapy took as their motto Epictetus’s sentence that “men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of things.”

The most telling evidence against the revival comes from the Stoic texts themselves, for Epictetus told his students, “On no occasion call yourself a philosopher, and do not speak much among the uninstructed about theorems … but do that which follows from them,” and he gave them the image of the sheep, which “do not vomit up their grass and show to the shepherds how much they have eaten; but … they produce externally wool and milk.” Marcus Aurelius wrote his Meditations for no one but himself. A Stoicism that is announced daily to nine hundred thousand subscribers is, by these standards, a contradiction, and the Daily Stoic itself once quoted Epictetus’s passage on the sheep in order to praise leaders who “aren’t on social media marketing themselves,” adding admiringly that “somehow even their posture—ramrod straight—tells you they are firm and full of conviction.” The virtue has become a visible bearing, a posture that tells.

No critic I have found calls the modern image of Stoic calm a fetish, but I think the word is the right one, and three classic accounts of the fetish show why. For Marx, the commodity is “a definite social relation between men, that assumes, in their eyes, the fantastic form of a relation between things,” and value turns every product into “a social hieroglyphic”; the Stoic ethic was social through and through, as Marcus says in a line the revival rarely quotes, “That which is not good for the swarm, neither is it good for the bee,” and it now returns to its owner as a thing, a medallion or a morning email. For Freud, the fetish is a substitute that lets a man keep believing in something he knows to be missing, and the calm face, or the coin in the pocket, stands in for the parts of Stoicism that have been lost, its providence, its community and its good feelings, while allowing the owner to disavow the loss. And film theory since Laura Mulvey has described how the camera’s look turns a body into something satisfying in itself, and how, when the body is a man’s, the look must be disguised, motivated by a fight or excused by a wound. The impassive male body of John Wick, cut and soaked in the rain, or of the Driver in his armour of satin, is offered to be looked at under cover of a revenge story.

The thinker who joins these threads most directly to antiquity is Theodor Adorno, who with Max Horkheimer wrote Dialectic of Enlightenment during the Second World War, and whose reading of the Odyssey makes Odysseus the first modern self. In their account Odysseus survives by mastering his own nature, and the emblem of that mastery is the episode of the Sirens, in which he has himself bound to the mast so that he can hear their song without yielding to it, while his oarsmen, their ears stopped with wax, row on. From this they draw a sentence that could stand over the whole history of the strong, silent man: “The history of civilization is the history of the introversion of sacrifice. In other words: the history of renunciation.” Of Odysseus enduring the suitors in silence they write that “Justice is restrained revenge,” and those four words could stand as the plot of every revenge film. They called Stoicism “the bourgeois philosophy,” which helps the privileged, faced with the suffering of others, to harden themselves against whatever threatens them, and self-help Stoicism, read in their light, asks its customer to be Odysseus at the mast and the oarsmen at once, to hear the song of a better life while rowing harder.

The Price of Silence

The Western has always half known that its hero’s silence can be read both ways, for it gives him the upper hand and spares him scrutiny, since a man who explains nothing can never be shown to be wrong, and behind it may lie not depth but a self that has given up on being heard, the mirror image of the silence the genre asked of its women. The pleasures of the type are real all the same, and a viewer can want them and not want them at once.

This double reading also suggests why the type appeals so strongly to men, although, as far as I can find, no one has studied male viewers of these heroes directly. The male viewer finds in the hero a more complete and more powerful version of himself, and a boy watching a Western studies how a man carries himself under fire, but there is also the wish to be understood without having to speak, which the films grant their heroes again and again, in the woman or the child who reads the silent man correctly, and which they extend to every viewer, who is let in on what the hero will not say.

Psychologists began to name the cost in the 1970s, when the inexpressive American male, strong, silent and two-fisted, was first described as a problem rather than an ideal, and therapists have since noticed how easily the impassive manner of traditional masculinity shades into the demeanour of depression, although research on men’s difficulty in putting feelings into words finds the difference between men and women real but small.

Adorno, in a lecture of 1966 on what education should learn from Auschwitz, attacked the ideal of hardness in terms that bear directly on the screen hero: “The idea that virility consists in the maximum degree of endurance long ago became a screen-image for masochism that, as psychology has demonstrated, aligns itself all too easily with sadism,” and the man who is hard with himself, he added, claims the right to be hard with others and avenges on them the pain he was never allowed to show. Seneca had already seen the trap from the other side, writing that the man who grieves with courage is called “undutiful and savage-hearted,” and the man who breaks down “womanish and weak,” so that there is no way to mourn that custom will approve. The films themselves have always known what the silence costs, and their most memorable images are of its price: Ennis Del Mar with his forehead pressed to an alley wall, and a man in Manchester who can only say that he cannot beat it.

Homecoming

Looking back over the ancient sources and the modern films together, I am struck by how rarely the strong, silent man stands alone. Odysseus hides his tears under his cloak from a hall full of strangers, and the poet tells us of them all the same; Seneca’s Cato stands upright amid the ruins as a spectacle for the gods; Mansfield’s silent type “needs to have his silence noticed — spoken of — by someone else”; the Daily Stoic admires a posture that “tells you” what the man within believes; and Machiavelli reminds the prince that “everyone sees how you appear.” The silence of the type is never self-sufficient but always seen and always narrated, and an ideal of perfect self-possession turns out to depend, at every point, on an audience. That is why the film camera suits him so well, and why the self-help industry could sell him back to men as a bearing to adopt rather than a life to live.

There is an older and kinder reading of silence in the Stoic tradition, which the Roman teacher Musonius Rufus put in a single sentence, that “the greatest admiration gives rise, not to words, but to silence.” In his view silence belongs first to the audience, to those who watch a good act and find nothing adequate to say, rather than to the man who performs it. If the strong, silent type has a lasting claim on us, it may be this one, that the best of these films leave their viewers silent at the end, watching a marshal’s star fall into the dirt.

But the poem in which the patient hero first appears does not end in silence, for when Odysseus has killed the suitors, and Penelope has tested him with the secret of the bed he built with his own hands and known him at last, Athena holds back the dawn to lengthen their night, and “when the two had had their fill of the joy of love, they took delight in tales, speaking each to the other.” She tells him what she endured among the suitors, and he tells her all that he suffered and all that he made others suffer, beginning with the first battle of his voyage home, and “she was glad to listen, nor did sweet sleep fall upon her eyelids, till he had told all the tale.” The man who swallowed every insult in silence among his enemies talks through the night with the one he loves, and that, more than any feat with the bow, is what the screen hero cannot do: to keep his silence for his enemies, and to come home and say what his deeds have meant to someone who wants to hear it.
