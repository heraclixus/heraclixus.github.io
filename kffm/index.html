Improving Function Space Flow Matching with Kernel Optimal Transport

kFFM replaces the random noise–data pairing of Functional Flow Matching with entropic optimal transport under a kernel-induced cost, the coupling behind the Hilbert Sinkhorn Divergence: a signature kernel for paths, a Sobolev or Euclidean RBF for fields. Same network, same loss, 5–7% more training time, and better samples on eight time-series and PDE benchmarks.

Fred Xu · November 18, 2026 · 47 min read

Paper ↗Code ↗

This post introduces our NeurIPS 2026 paper, “Improving Function Space Flow Matching with Kernel Optimal Transport,” written with Thomas Markovich of Block, Barbora Barancikova of Imperial College London and Yizhou Sun of UCLA. Many scientific datasets are made of functions, such as temperature curves, gene-activity time series, volatility paths and snapshots of a fluid. Functional Flow Matching (FFM) learns to generate such data by gradually turning random functions, the noise, into realistic ones, but during training it pairs each noise sample with a data sample at random. Our method, kernel Functional Flow Matching (kFFM), pairs each noise sample with a data sample that already resembles it, by solving a small optimal-transport problem in every training batch under a notion of distance designed for functions: a signature kernel for paths, and an RBF kernel on a derivative-aware Sobolev norm for fields. Nothing else changes, neither the neural network nor the training loss, and training takes 5–7% longer at the largest resolution we train. We prove that this kernel pairing is well-posed and bounded on general function spaces, split its gap to standard optimal transport into interpretable terms, and show that it is stable as the grid is refined. On eight time-series and PDE benchmarks, kFFM has the lowest MMD of all compared methods on every dataset and beats FFM with seed-paired significance on all eight (on three of them while also using a different noise distribution), and the gains hold under non-kernel metrics and on a turbulent Navier–Stokes benchmark.

Why generate functions?

Some data are best thought of as functions rather than as lists of numbers. A weather station’s average temperature over the year is a curve, and so is the activity of a gene over time, or a country’s population from 1950 to 2018; the variance of a stock in a stochastic volatility model traces a rough, jagged path; and the vorticity of a two-dimensional fluid is a field, one number at every point of a square.

A generative model for such data learns their distribution and can then produce new, realistic samples, such as plausible temperature profiles, volatility scenarios or fluid states, and two properties make this setting different from generating images or vectors. The first is that the grid is not the object: the left panel above shows one curve at three resolutions, and a good model should learn the curve rather than the grid, so that it can be trained at one resolution and sampled at another. The second is that geometry matters, since two functions can be close or far apart in ways a flat list of grid values does not capture, one being a slightly shifted copy of the other, or the same shape with fast wiggles on top, or a path with the same overall trend but a very different roughness.

Our paper improves one step of a strong existing method for this problem, and it draws on more mathematics than most machine-learning papers: function spaces, probability measures on them, optimal transport, kernels and path signatures. The first half of this post builds these up one at a time, each with pictures, and the second half uses them to explain kFFM, its theory and its results. Readers who already know a topic can skip its section, and the method itself is summarized in the section “kFFM on one page.”

Functions as points in a space

To do geometry and probability with functions, we treat each function as a single point in a space. Random functions and Gaussian measures

A generative model for functions is a probability distribution on a function space, and probability in infinite dimensions has one big surprise that shapes everything that follows: there is no uniform measure, and so there are no densities. In Rd\mathbb{R}^dRd a probability density p(x)p(x)p(x) is defined relative to volume, the Lebesgue measure, which is the same everywhere, but on an infinite-dimensional space no such measure exists. The reason fits in one sentence: the unit ball contains infinitely many disjoint balls of radius 1/41/41/4, centered at en/2e_n/2en​/2 for each basis vector ene_nen​, and a translation-invariant measure would have to give them all the same mass, so either everything has mass zero or the unit ball has infinite mass. A “density” then only makes sense relative to another measure: μ\muμ has a density dμ/dνd\mu/d\nudμ/dν with respect to ν\nuν, its Radon–Nikodym derivative, exactly when μ\muμ is absolutely continuous with respect to ν\nuν, written μ≪ν\mu \ll \nuμ≪ν, meaning that every set of ν\nuν-probability zero also has μ\muμ-probability zero.

The best-understood distributions on a Hilbert space are Gaussian. A Gaussian measure N(m,C)\mathcal{N}(m, C)N(m,C) is a distribution under which every linear measurement ⟨f,h⟩\langle f, h \rangle⟨f,h⟩ is a one-dimensional Gaussian, with mean ⟨m,h⟩\langle m, h \rangle⟨m,h⟩ and variance ⟨Ch,h⟩\langle C h, h \rangle⟨Ch,h⟩. White noise is the case C=IC = IC=I, with λn=1\lambda_n = 1λn​=1 for every nnn. The sum diverges, so white noise is not a random element of L2L^2L2: on a fixed grid it looks harmless, but its variance is spread evenly over all modes, so it gets rougher as the grid is refined and never settles down to a function. FFM uses GP noise, while kFFM treats the noise distribution, called the base measure, as part of the configuration chosen for each dataset, a GP where the data are smooth and white noise on the grid where the data are rougher than a smooth GP prior, as in the Navier–Stokes family.

In finite dimensions any two non-degenerate Gaussians have densities with respect to each other, but in infinite dimensions this fails dramatically, for by the Feldman–Hájek theorem two Gaussian measures on a Hilbert space are either equivalent, each absolutely continuous with respect to the other, or mutually singular, living on disjoint sets, with nothing in between. The reason shows up already in high dimensions: under N(0,Id)\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)N(0,Id​) the normalized squared length ∥x∥2/d\|x\|^2/d∥x∥2/d concentrates at 1, under N(0,1.21 Id)\mathcal{N}(0, 1.21\, I_d)N(0,1.21Id​) at 1.21, and as ddd grows the overlap vanishes, so that in infinite dimensions scaling the covariance by any constant other than 1 produces a singular measure.

The Cameron–Martin theorem treats shifts. Moving N(0,C)\mathcal{N}(0, C)N(0,C) by a function hhh gives an equivalent measure exactly when hhh belongs to the Cameron–Martin space C1/2(H)C^{1/2}(\mathcal{H})C1/2(H), the shifts with a finite Cameron–Martin norm ∑nhn2/λn\sum_n h_n^2 / \lambda_n∑n​hn2​/λn​, where hn=⟨h,en⟩h_n = \langle h, e_n \ranglehn​=⟨h,en​⟩, and the density is then exp⁡{h^(x)−12∥h∥CM2}\exp\{\hat h(x) - \tfrac12 \|h\|^2_{\mathrm{CM}}\}exp{h^(x)−21​∥h∥CM2​}, where h^\hat hh^ is a linear functional determined by hhh. For a Brownian bridge, the Cameron–Martin space consists of the functions that vanish at both ends and have a square-integrable derivative, so that a smooth shift is allowed and a rough one is not. Samples of the measure itself almost surely lie outside its Cameron–Martin space, since Brownian paths have no derivative at all, so a sample of the noise is never an allowed shift.

Three consequences recur in the rest of the post. Many tools of optimal transport in Rd\mathbb{R}^dRd use densities with respect to volume, which are not available on function spaces, so we will need a formulation that works with samples and costs alone. FFM’s training loss is justified by an absolute-continuity condition, which requires that at each time ttt each conditional path distribution be absolutely continuous with respect to the mixture of all of them; a sufficient condition is that the data lie in the Cameron–Martin space of the prior, with σmin⁡>0\sigma_{\min} > 0σmin​>0, and when the data are rougher than the prior this degrades, which is one reason to match the roughness of the base measure to the data, and why white noise is selected on the Navier–Stokes family. And kFFM changes the pairing but inherits this structure, which, as we will see, requires the entropic regularization of optimal transport.

Which noise sample goes with which data sample? The standard answer is any, at random, which is the independent coupling π=μ0⊗μ1\pi = \mu_0 \otimes \mu_1π=μ0​⊗μ1​, but flow-matching theory says that the learned flow carries the noise distribution to the data distribution for any coupling, so we are free to choose a better one. Random pairing has a cost: straight lines heading to very different data points pass through the same location at the same time, the network can output only one velocity there, and since the minimizer of a squared loss is the average of the targets, it learns a compromise that points toward none of them. Optimal-transport pairing, introduced for flow matching by Tong and colleagues and by Pooladian and colleagues, pairs each noise point with a nearby data point instead, so that the lines stop crossing and the targets at each location agree.

The problem: random pairs make a hard regression

Writing the bridge with an explicit noise partner shows where the difficulty lies. kFFM uses the same path as FFM, gt=tf1+σtf0g_t = t f_1 + \sigma_t f_0gt​=tf1​+σt​f0​, with the pair (f0,f1)(f_0, f_1)(f0​,f1​) drawn from a coupling π\piπ and the target velocity f1−(1−σmin⁡)f0f_1 - (1 - \sigma_{\min}) f_0f1​−(1−σmin​)f0​, which for the independent coupling is exactly FFM. Up to the tiny σmin⁡\sigma_{\min}σmin​ term, the network regresses the residual u=f1−f0u = f_1 - f_0u=f1​−f0​ between a data function and its noise partner, and under random pairing this residual is the difference of two unrelated draws, a large target that varies a lot from pair to pair, so that the network has to discover the shared structure of the dataset and match each particular instance at the same time. The fix suggested by the two-dimensional picture is to pair each data function with a noise function that is already close to it in a meaningful sense, so that the bridge mostly transports the remaining difference, and the cover figure at the top of this post shows the effect on a synthetic dataset, where the kFFM coupling makes the residuals about 21% smaller on average.

In finite dimensions OT pairing is well established, but in function space it meets two obstacles. The first, as we have seen, is that OT tools relying on densities are unavailable. The second is the choice of distance. The obvious shortcut is to flatten each discretized function into a long vector and use the ordinary squared distance, which is what a direct port of finite-dimensional OT flow matching does and is one of our baselines, CFM-OT(L2L^2L2), but a flat distance treats grid values as unrelated coordinates and does not see smoothness, derivatives or path roughness, which are exactly what distinguish one function-valued sample from another. The next four sections develop the tools that address both obstacles: optimal transport computed from samples, kernels that encode the geometry, the divergence that combines them, and the signature kernel for paths.

Optimal transport

Given two distributions μ\muμ and ν\nuν and a cost c(x,y)c(x, y)c(x,y) of moving xxx to yyy, Monge asked in 1781 for the map TTT that pushes μ\muμ onto ν\nuν, written T#μ=νT_\# \mu = \nuT#​μ=ν and meaning that if X∼μX \sim \muX∼μ then T(X)∼νT(X) \sim \nuT(X)∼ν, at the least average cost ∫c(x,T(x)) dμ(x)\int c(x, T(x))\, d\mu(x)∫c(x,T(x))dμ(x). Paths and the signature kernel

Time series are paths, and paths have structure that neither L2L^2L2 nor Sobolev norms capture well, namely the order in which things happen and how rough the path is. Combining Theorems 2 and 3 bounds the gap between the objective the model actually computes and continuum quadratic optimal transport in HkH^kHk by four interpretable terms, kernel mismatch, entropic blur, covering and discretization, of which only the last vanishes as the resolution grows.

One more property matters: kFFM inherits FFM’s foundations. FFM’s loss rests on absolute-continuity relations between Gaussian measures, and with a bounded cost the entropic plan has a density with respect to the independent coupling, exp⁡((φ⊕ψ−cκ)/ϵ)\exp((\varphi \oplus \psi - c_\kappa)/\epsilon)exp((φ⊕ψ−cκ​)/ϵ), with bounded potentials, so this density is bounded above and away from zero. The plan is therefore equivalent to the independent coupling, its conditionals are equivalent to the prior, and every absolute-continuity relation that FFM relies on transfers to kFFM. This is where entropic regularization matters beyond computation, since an unregularized OT plan can be singular with respect to the independent coupling, and the argument would fail.

Finite-dimensional OT flow matching is often motivated by straighter paths and hence cheaper sampling, but we make no straightness or sampling-efficiency claim. kFFM changes only which endpoints are paired, the path between them is the same straight-line template, and the geometry of Gaussian measures on Hilbert spaces, Bures–Wasserstein rather than flat, does not inherit the Euclidean picture behind that intuition, as the paper’s appendix states precisely.

Results

We used four sequence datasets, AEMET (the mean annual temperature profile at 73 Spanish weather stations, 365 points), gene expression, economic series (population, GDP and labor) and volatility paths from the Heston model, and four PDE datasets, the 1D Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equation, the 2D incompressible Navier–Stokes equation and stochastic versions of both, with a turbulent Navier–Stokes benchmark added at the end. The baselines are FFM; functional diffusion (DDPM); a score-based denoising diffusion operator (DDO with NCSN noise scales); an adversarial neural operator (GANO); and CFM-OT(L2L^2L2), which uses the same Sinkhorn solver and neural operator as kFFM but applies OT to the flattened grid with the raw L2L^2L2 cost and white noise.

The main metric is the MMD with an RBF kernel, computed with the unbiased estimator, so that a tiny or negative value means “indistinguishable from the data at this sample size,” and percentage gains there are not meaningful. Because an RBF-based metric could favor an RBF-based pairing, we also report non-kernel metrics (the sliced Wasserstein distance and the marginal W1W_1W1​), structural diagnostics (the autocorrelation error for sequences and the log-spectrum error for PDEs) and physics diagnostics on Navier–Stokes. Results are over 10 seeds (20 for the comparison with FFM on four datasets), and all methods share seeds, so comparisons are paired by seed.

For each dataset, the cost and the base measure of kFFM are chosen on a held-out validation split by sliced Wasserstein distance, a criterion that does not involve the kernels being compared. The selected configurations follow the rule given above: the signature kernel with a GP base on path-like data, except short-horizon Heston, where the top configurations are statistically indistinguishable and the rule picks the RBF cost with white noise; the Sobolev-RBF cost with a GP base on the KdV family; and the Euclidean RBF cost with white noise on the Navier–Stokes family, whose rough fields a smooth GP prior matches poorly.

Distributional quality

kFFM has the lowest MMD of all six methods on all eight datasets. Where the strongest baseline is not at the noise floor, the improvement over it is 74% on stochastic KdV, 60% on Economy and on gene expression, 34% on stochastic Navier–Stokes and 8% on Navier–Stokes, and relative to CFM-OT(L2L^2L2), the same solver and network with a flat L2L^2L2 cost, kFFM lowers MMD by 75% on gene expression and 66% on Economy.

kFFM improves on FFM on every dataset at the 0.05 level under both tests, and on the four datasets with the largest gaps it wins on every seed. The evidence is not equally strong everywhere, however: on AEMET, Heston, KdV and stochastic KdV the MMD values sit near zero and the p-values are between 0.011 and 0.039, significant but modest.

On the same runs, kFFM also improves the sliced Wasserstein distance and the marginal W1W_1W1​ over FFM on the four datasets of the paper’s non-kernel table, by 18–20% on gene expression and Economy, where each comparison is significant when paired by seed, and by 2.9–4.1× on Navier–Stokes and stochastic Navier–Stokes; for the signature configurations the pairing kernel and the evaluation kernel already differ. The structural diagnostics show what MMD understates, for CFM-OT(L2L^2L2) matches pointwise values reasonably well but has 105× and 66× larger autocorrelation error than kFFM with the signature kernel on Economy and gene expression, and function-level structure is where the kernel pairing helps most.

Bounded cost or function-space geometry?

Both effects isolated in the toy examples show up on real data, on different data classes.

On paths, geometry is what matters, for no Euclidean cost recovers the gains of the signature kernel: on gene expression, kFFM with the raw L2L^2L2 cost reaches 20.0 and with the RBF cost 20.7, against 7.11 with the signature kernel, and on Economy 7.55 and 7.60 against 2.89, so the signature kernel genuinely re-ranks pairs relative to L2L^2L2. On smooth fields, boundedness is what matters, since the RBF cost is a monotone transform of L2L^2L2, with the same ranking of pairs and the outliers compressed, and on smooth Navier–Stokes fields the plans under the two costs nearly coincide, at a total variation of at most 0.02. What the bounded cost buys there is conditioning, robustness to ϵ\epsilonϵ and faster Sinkhorn, and it matches or improves the unbounded cost at the same base measure.

A turbulent stress test

The Navier–Stokes benchmark above is smooth, the regime where our theory is informative. To test the rough regime, where Theorem 2 is weakest, we used the turbulent 2D Navier–Stokes benchmark of the FNO paper (viscosity 10−510^{-5}10−5, Reynolds number about 2000) and compared seven methods over five seeds, with the same network and training budget for all. Besides MMD and the sliced Wasserstein distance, we measured physics diagnostics: the distributions of vorticity and enstrophy, the skewness and kurtosis of the vorticity, and the energy spectrum.

kFFM has the best value on five of the seven metrics and ties for the best MMD, while CFM-OT(L2L^2L2) at the same white-noise base overlaps with kFFM within one standard deviation. OT-paired flow matching is far more robust than the score-based and diffusion alternatives, with 23–32× better vorticity-distribution W1W_1W1​, 5.7–6.9× better enstrophy W1W_1W1​, and 17–43× smaller kurtosis error. Against FFM with its GP prior, kFFM improves every metric except kurtosis, where the two tie, with seed-paired significance (its MMD, for example, is 73× lower, p=3×10−4p = 3 \times 10^{-4}p=3×10−4). This gain combines two changes, however: the three methods that use the GP base are indistinguishable from each other, so in this regime the white-noise base does most of the work, and at that base the kernel and raw L2L^2L2 costs tie. The turbulent benchmark therefore supports OT-paired flow matching with a white-noise base as the strongest current option, without singling out the kernel cost.

Cost and sensitivity

The per-batch Sinkhorn solve adds 5–7% to end-to-end training time at the largest resolution we train, 128×128128 \times 128128×128 Navier–Stokes. Its cost grows with the square of the batch size but does not depend on the model size or the resolution, so its share shrinks as the network grows; peak memory for the pairing is 80–151 MB, 1.5–9% of the network’s own peak, and one pairing takes about as long as one network step. The bounded RBF cost makes Sinkhorn about 8 times faster than the raw L2L^2L2 cost at b=512b = 512b=512 (9 against 73 ms per batch) and training 15–25% faster end-to-end, and giving FFM the extra time as additional epochs does not close the gap.

Results are indistinguishable within seed-to-seed variation for RBF bandwidths between 0.1 and 2. With bounded costs they barely move across the Sinkhorn regularization values we swept, whereas the raw L2L^2L2 cost degrades by about 10× at ϵ≥0.5\epsilon \ge 0.5ϵ≥0.5 on long volatility paths, and they are unchanged across the signature kernel’s dyadic order (0 to 3) and static bandwidth (0.1 to 5). The kernel family is the choice that matters: match the kernel to the data class, set the bandwidth by the median heuristic, and validate with a non-kernel metric on held-out data. The properties the theorems require, boundedness and characteristicness, hold for every kernel we consider, so the theory constrains the family only weakly and the selection rule does the rest.

Limitations

The theory certifies the pairing, not the samples: it establishes well-posedness, an error decomposition and discretization invariance, but says nothing about straightness or sampling speed, on which we make no claim, and the gains in sample quality remain an empirical finding. Rough data are where the theory says least, for on the turbulent benchmark the distances between noise and data functions more than double relative to the standard benchmark (a median of 91 against 40), which pushes the RBF cost into saturation, so that the mismatch term grows and the covering term of Theorem 2 is large for any practical ϵ\epsilonϵ, and there the empirical results carry the method. Turbulence is still open, since the failures sit mainly with the score-based and diffusion baselines, but all methods, ours included, leave headroom (the best sliced Wasserstein distance is 0.19), and in this regime the base measure rather than the pairing does most of the work. The signature kernel is memory-hungry on long paths, its memory growing with path length (0.63 GB at 100 time points against 12.5 GB at 512), so very long paths fall back to RBF or L2L^2L2 costs. And some gains are modest, for on four datasets the MMD values are near the noise floor and the paired p-values are between 0.011 and 0.039.

Takeaways

Pairing is a design choice: flow matching works with any coupling of noise and data, random pairing is the default rather than a requirement, and pairing each data function with a noise function that already resembles it leaves the network a smaller, more structured residual to learn. In function space, transport needs samples rather than densities, for without a uniform reference measure densities and many classical OT tools are unavailable, but minibatch entropic OT needs only pairwise costs, and its plan keeps the absolute-continuity structure that FFM relies on. A kernel is the way to choose the geometry, bringing prior knowledge about the data into the pairing, since bounded costs keep Sinkhorn well conditioned, Sobolev norms see derivatives, and the signature kernel sees the order and roughness of paths, while the resulting Hilbert Sinkhorn Divergence sits between kernel OT and MMD. And the change is cheap and leaves everything else alone, adding 5–7% to training at our largest resolution without touching the network, the loss or the sampler. If you generate time series, paths or PDE fields with flow matching, we hope kFFM is a useful drop-in change, and the code is on GitHub.
